# Market-Behavior Paper Manifest

All eleven papers were accessed and reviewed in full on 2026-07-31. Findings
below are the authors' results, not promises of present-day predictability or
profit. Page counts refer to the reviewed artifact and may differ from journal
pagination.

## B1: Prospect Theory: An Analysis of Decision under Risk

- **Authors/year/venue:** Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky, 1979,
  *Econometrica* 47(2).
- **Source:** https://doi.org/10.2307/1914185; reviewed author-hosted copy:
  https://web.mit.edu/curhan/www/docs/Articles/15341_Readings/Behavioral_Decision_Theory/Kahneman_Tversky_1979_Prospect_theory.pdf;
  29 pages; full text reviewed: yes.
- **Finding:** Choices were reference dependent and asymmetric across gains and
  losses, with diminishing sensitivity and nonlinear decision weighting in the
  reported problems.
- **Limit:** The paper uses stylized, mostly hypothetical choice problems and
  explicitly leaves open generalization to market behavior.
- **Agent impact:** Treat reference points and loss aversion as scenario
  mechanisms only; identify the reference and observable behavior before use.

## B2: A Unified Theory of Underreaction, Momentum Trading, and Overreaction

- **Authors/year/venue:** Harrison Hong and Jeremy C. Stein, 1999, *Journal of
  Finance* 54(6).
- **Source:** https://doi.org/10.1111/0022-1082.00184; reviewed author copy:
  https://users.ssc.wisc.edu/~cengel/IMF/HongSteinJF1999.pdf; 43 pages;
  full text reviewed: yes.
- **Finding:** In the model, gradual private-information diffusion creates
  underreaction; momentum traders exploit it and can eventually produce
  overreaction, especially where information diffuses slowly.
- **Limit:** This is a stylized heterogeneous-agent model, not identification of
  a universal price path; parameters and information structure are decisive.
- **Agent impact:** Compare novelty, analyst coverage, diffusion, price path, and
  volume before choosing continuation, reversal, or no behavioral effect.

## B3: All That Glitters: The Effect of Attention and News on Buying Behavior

- **Authors/year/venue:** Brad M. Barber and Terrance Odean, 2008, *Review of
  Financial Studies* 21(2).
- **Source:** https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/hhm079; reviewed author copy:
  https://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/odean/Papers%20current%20versions/AllThatGlitters_RFS_2008.pdf;
  34 pages; full text reviewed: yes.
- **Finding:** Individual investors in the samples were net buyers of
  attention-grabbing stocks proxied by news, extreme one-day returns, and high
  volume; institutional samples, especially value managers, differed.
- **Limit:** Historical brokerage and institutional samples do not identify all
  investors, current channels, causality, or profitable direction.
- **Agent impact:** Measure attention separately from tone and direction, and
  distinguish retail-like flow from professional or systematic participation.

## B4: Investor Sentiment and the Cross-Section of Stock Returns

- **Authors/year/venue:** Malcolm Baker and Jeffrey Wurgler, 2006, *Journal of
  Finance* 61(4).
- **Source:** https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.2006.00885.x; reviewed author
  copy:
  https://www.hbs.edu/ris/Publication%20Files/sentiment_5e81d758-b344-4c0b-bf6a-3eccbf4cf1b6.pdf;
  36 pages; full text reviewed: yes.
- **Finding:** Beginning-period sentiment proxies conditioned the historical
  cross-section of later returns, especially for young, small, volatile,
  unprofitable, non-dividend, distressed, or extreme-growth stocks.
- **Limit:** Composite proxies, correlated characteristics, risk explanations,
  causality, and historical-sample transfer remain material concerns.
- **Agent impact:** Use sentiment only with valuation uncertainty and
  limits-to-arbitrage context; never apply a universal high-sentiment rule.

## B5: Learning to Be Overconfident

- **Authors/year/venue:** Simon Gervais and Terrance Odean, 2001, *Review of
  Financial Studies* 14(1).
- **Source:** https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/14.1.1; reviewed author copy:
  https://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/odean/papers/learning/ovcon.pdf; 56 pages;
  full text reviewed: yes.
- **Finding:** In the model, self-attribution after success makes relatively
  inexperienced successful traders overconfident, increasing volume,
  volatility, and aggressive trading while reducing expected profits.
- **Limit:** The learning and market structure are theoretical; supporting
  volume patterns have alternative explanations and do not identify live
  traders' beliefs.
- **Agent impact:** Treat success-followed-by-turnover as a testable mechanism,
  not a diagnosis; require volume and participant evidence plus alternatives.

## B6: Are Investors Reluctant to Realize Their Losses?

- **Authors/year/venue:** Terrance Odean, 1998, *Journal of Finance* 53(5).
- **Source:** https://doi.org/10.1111/0022-1082.00072; reviewed author copy:
  https://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/odean/papers/disposition/disposit.pdf; 24
  pages; full text reviewed: yes.
- **Finding:** Investors in 10,000 brokerage accounts realized gains more readily
  than losses except in December; rebalancing, costs, and subsequent returns did
  not justify the pattern.
- **Limit:** One historical discount broker, incomplete household wealth, taxes,
  and observational design limit market-wide and current generalization.
- **Agent impact:** Consider reference-price supply effects and tax timing only
  when ownership, cost-basis, volume, or realized-flow evidence supports them.

## B7: Do Investors Trade Too Much?

- **Authors/year/venue:** Terrance Odean, 1999, *American Economic Review*
  89(5).
- **Source:** https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.89.5.1279; reviewed author copy:
  https://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/odean/papers/overconf/DoInvestors.pdf; 20
  pages; full text reviewed: yes.
- **Finding:** In the brokerage sample, purchased securities underperformed sold
  securities and trading reduced returns even before costs in key comparisons;
  attention and overconfidence were proposed explanations.
- **Limit:** The sample is historical and observational, and some behavioral
  explanations in the discussion were explicitly conjectural rather than tested.
- **Agent impact:** Penalize turnover and demand an edge after costs; do not use
  trading intensity alone as proof of overconfidence.

## B8: Expectations of Returns and Expected Returns

- **Authors/year/venue:** Robin Greenwood and Andrei Shleifer, 2014, *Review of
  Financial Studies* 27(3).
- **Source:** https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/hht082; reviewed NBER version:
  https://www.nber.org/papers/w18686.pdf; 52 pages; full text reviewed: yes.
- **Finding:** Across six surveys, investor return expectations were positively
  related to past returns and market levels and negatively related to several
  model-based expected-return measures; flows aligned with reported beliefs.
- **Limit:** Survey populations, aggregate monthly frequency, measurement error,
  and reliance on model-based expected returns constrain security-level use.
- **Agent impact:** Use surveys as direct belief evidence when temporally and
  demographically relevant; distinguish expected price gains from expected
  returns implied by valuation models.

## B9: Giving Content to Investor Sentiment: The Role of Media in the Stock Market

- **Authors/year/venue:** Paul C. Tetlock, 2007, *Journal of Finance* 62(3).
- **Source:** https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.2007.01232.x; reviewed author
  copy: https://www.columbia.edu/~pt2238/papers/Tetlock_Media_Sentiment_JF.pdf;
  51 pages; full text reviewed: yes.
- **Finding:** Pessimistic language in a historical *Wall Street Journal* column
  forecast temporary downward price pressure and reversal, while extreme tone
  was associated with unusually high volume.
- **Limit:** One media column, historical period, dictionary bag-of-words method,
  turnover costs, and causal ambiguity prevent direct current trading use.
- **Agent impact:** Separate news facts, language, reach, and observed response;
  test reversal and cost hypotheses rather than assuming negative tone is value.

## B10: Do Stock Prices Move Too Much to Be Justified by Subsequent Changes in Dividends?

- **Authors/year/venue:** Robert J. Shiller, 1981, *American Economic Review*
  71(3).
- **Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w0456.pdf; 40 pages;
  full text reviewed: yes.
- **Finding:** Detrended aggregate U.S. stock-price movements were much larger
  than could be reconciled with subsequently observed real dividends under the
  paper's simple efficient-markets benchmark.
- **Limit:** Formal rejection depends on maintained dividend-process,
  discount-rate, terminal-value, detrending, and rare-event assumptions; the
  exercise does not identify a behavioral cause.
- **Agent impact:** Treat narrative or discount-rate uncertainty as a candidate
  explanation for wide valuation ranges, not as proof of irrationality.

## B11: The Impact of Institutional Trading on Stock Prices

- **Authors/year/venue:** Josef Lakonishok, Andrei Shleifer, and Robert W.
  Vishny, 1992, *Journal of Financial Economics* 32(1).
- **Source:** https://doi.org/10.1016/0304-405X(92)90023-Q; reviewed NBER
  version: https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w3846/w3846.pdf; 32
  pages; full text reviewed: yes.
- **Finding:** In 769 pension funds, evidence of herding and positive-feedback
  trading was weak overall and somewhat stronger in small stocks, with little
  evidence in the largest stocks.
- **Limit:** Historical pension funds, quarterly holdings, and a U.S. equity
  sample cannot rule out market-wide, intraperiod, retail, or other herding.
- **Agent impact:** Require participant- and frequency-specific flow evidence;
  never infer universal institutional herding from correlated price moves.
